# French New Wave

## Purpose

This skill governs the application of French New Wave (La Nouvelle Vague) as a complete cinematic movement for Seedance.

French New Wave is not simply handheld cinematography, jump cuts, black-and-white imagery, Paris streets, or low-budget aesthetics. It is a filmmaking philosophy centered on personal expression, spontaneity, experimentation, and creative freedom.

When activated, this skill should transform the visual language into an author-driven cinematic experience where the filmmaker's personality becomes visible through camera movement, editing choices, composition, performance, and narrative structure.

The result should feel like a filmmaker exploring reality with curiosity and individual perspective rather than following established cinematic conventions.

# Cinematic Law

French New Wave makes the presence of the filmmaker visible.

The camera is not an invisible recording device.

It is curious.
It reacts.
It explores.
It expresses a point of view.

Every creative decision should feel like a personal artistic choice rather than a neutral cinematic convention.

Imperfection is not a limitation.

Imperfection becomes evidence of creative freedom.

# When to Use

Activate this skill whenever the user explicitly requests French New Wave or when the creative objective naturally aligns with experimental, personal, and spontaneous filmmaking.

Typical indicators include:

- coming-of-age stories
- youthful romance
- urban exploration
- independent cinema
- artistic expression
- existential reflection
- personal storytelling
- unconventional narratives
- intimate character studies
- philosophical drama
- playful cinematic experimentation
- auteur-driven filmmaking

Do not activate this skill solely because the user requests:

- handheld camera
- jump cuts
- black-and-white imagery
- street photography
- retro European aesthetics

These elements alone do not constitute French New Wave.

# Camera

The camera should feel like an active participant.

Favor:

- handheld movement
- spontaneous reframing
- long tracking shots
- unexpected camera choices
- casual zooms
- whip pans when emotionally motivated
- direct interaction with subjects
- unconventional viewpoints
- fluid movement through real locations

Avoid:

- invisible classical coverage
- perfectly controlled camera movement
- purely functional framing
- overly polished cinematic choreography

The camera should feel curious rather than precise.

---

# Editing

Editing should express freedom rather than simply preserve continuity.

Favor:

- jump cuts
- discontinuity editing
- playful temporal shifts
- unexpected transitions
- elliptical storytelling
- self-aware editing
- unconventional pacing

Avoid using editing techniques mechanically.

A jump cut should express thought, emotion, rhythm, or personality rather than exist as a stylistic decoration.

---

# Style Boundaries

Avoid reducing French New Wave to recognizable surface elements.

French New Wave is not defined solely by:

- jump cuts
- handheld cameras
- black-and-white photography
- Paris streets
- casual clothing
- improvised dialogue

Avoid:

- generic indie film aesthetics
- nostalgic retro styling without purpose
- polished commercial cinema
- artificial quirkiness
- random experimentation
- visual rebellion without emotional meaning

The goal is not to imitate the 1960s.

The goal is to preserve creative freedom and personal authorship.

---

# Integration

French New Wave should establish the dominant visual language whenever active.

It should control:

- camera personality
- editing rhythm
- narrative freedom
- performance style

Other skills may influence atmosphere, lighting, or production design but should not remove the movement's spontaneous and experimental character.

---

# Default Behavior

If French New Wave is neither explicitly requested nor naturally appropriate for the narrative, do not activate this skill.

Maintain visual neutrality and allow other cinematic movement skills to determine the overall style.

---

# Final Principle

French New Wave is the cinema of personal voice.

Its success is measured not by copying 1960s visual references, but by whether every creative decision creates the feeling that a unique filmmaker is actively discovering, questioning, and expressing the world through cinema.
