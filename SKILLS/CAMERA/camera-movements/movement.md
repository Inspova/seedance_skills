# Camera Movement

## Purpose

Camera movement should always support the story.

Movement is not decoration. Every motion should guide attention, reveal information, strengthen emotion, or improve spatial understanding.

If movement does not improve the scene, prefer a static camera.

---

## Push In

Description

The camera slowly moves toward the subject, increasing emotional intensity while maintaining natural perspective.

Film Language

A push in slowly moves the camera toward the subject, drawing viewers deeper into the scene or character's emotional state. This technique builds intensity, focuses attention, and creates engagement. Slow pushes feel contemplative, while faster pushes create urgency.

Best for

- emotional realization
- intimate dialogue
- dramatic discoveries
- internal conflict
- rising tension

Avoid when

- the scene should feel detached
- the framing is already extremely tight

Example

The camera slowly pushes toward the protagonist as they finally understand the truth.

---

## Pull Out

Description

The camera gradually moves away from the subject, revealing more of the surrounding environment.

Film Language

A pull out expands the audience's understanding of space while often reducing emotional intimacy. It can suggest isolation, vulnerability, freedom, or reveal the broader context surrounding a character.

Best for

- endings
- revealing scale
- emotional distance
- loneliness
- transitions

Avoid when

- maintaining intimacy is the priority

Example

The camera slowly pulls away to reveal the lone survivor standing in the vast ruined city.

---

## Dolly

Description

The camera physically moves through space on a smooth track or stabilized platform.

Film Language

Unlike zooming, a dolly changes the camera's physical position, creating natural perspective shifts and stronger spatial realism. Dolly movement feels immersive because foreground and background move relative to one another.

Best for

- following characters
- cinematic movement
- immersive scenes
- environmental exploration

Avoid when

- unnecessary movement distracts from dialogue

Example

The camera dollies alongside two characters walking through a crowded market.

---

## Tracking Shot

Description

The camera continuously follows a moving subject while preserving visual continuity.

Film Language

Tracking shots maintain audience connection with a subject in motion, allowing movement to unfold naturally while preserving spatial orientation. They create immersion without drawing attention to editing.

Best for

- walking scenes
- action
- sports
- conversations while moving

Avoid when

- static observation better serves the story

Example

The camera tracks beside a runner through a rainy street.

---

## Pan

Description

The camera rotates horizontally from a fixed position.

Film Language

A pan reveals information across a scene without changing camera position. It naturally guides attention, introduces new subjects, or follows movement across the frame.

Best for

- reveals
- following movement
- establishing geography
- conversations between multiple characters

Avoid when

- rapid movement may confuse orientation

Example

The camera slowly pans from an empty room to reveal someone waiting in the shadows.

---

## Tilt

Description

The camera rotates vertically from a fixed position.

Film Language

Tilting changes the viewer's understanding of height and scale. Upward tilts emphasize grandeur or power, while downward tilts reveal vulnerability or hidden information.

Best for

- architecture
- character reveals
- scale
- vertical movement

Avoid when

- horizontal movement communicates the scene more clearly

Example

The camera tilts upward from a character's feet to reveal towering armor.

---

## Crane

Description

The camera moves vertically through space using a crane, jib, or virtual equivalent.

Film Language

Crane shots create elegant perspective changes impossible with handheld operation. They are frequently used for dramatic reveals, endings, or establishing the relationship between subjects and environments.

Best for

- establishing shots
- finales
- revealing locations
- cinematic transitions

Avoid when

- intimate realism is desired

Example

The camera cranes upward as the crowd disperses, revealing the entire city square.

---

## Orbit

Description

The camera circles around a subject while continuously facing it.

Film Language

Orbiting emphasizes the importance of a subject while adding dynamic energy. It can create feelings of romance, tension, confusion, or triumph depending on speed and framing.

Best for

- dramatic confrontations
- romantic moments
- heroic reveals
- emotional climaxes

Avoid when

- the movement distracts from dialogue

Example

The camera slowly orbits around two characters as they finally embrace.

---

## Arc Shot

Description

The camera moves along a partial circular path around the subject.

Film Language

An arc shot introduces subtle perspective change without completing a full orbit. It maintains visual energy while preserving orientation and often feels more restrained than a full circle.

Best for

- dialogue
- introductions
- cinematic reveals

Avoid when

- completely static framing is preferable

Example

The camera gently arcs around the speaker during an emotional confession.

---

## Handheld

Description

The camera is intentionally unstable, reflecting natural human movement.

Film Language

Handheld cinematography creates immediacy, realism, and emotional proximity. Small imperfections in movement make viewers feel physically present within the scene.

Best for

- documentaries
- realism
- action
- chaos
- urgency

Avoid when

- elegance or precision is desired

Example

The handheld camera follows firefighters through a collapsing building.

---

## Static Camera

Description

The camera remains completely stationary.

Film Language

A static camera allows blocking, performance, and composition to carry the scene. It often creates confidence, realism, contemplation, or quiet observation by refusing unnecessary movement.

Best for

- dialogue
- suspense
- symmetrical composition
- contemplative scenes

Avoid when

- movement is necessary to clarify action

Example

The camera remains perfectly still as two strangers silently wait for the train.

---

## Zoom

Description

The focal length changes without moving the camera.

Film Language

Unlike a dolly, zooming alters magnification while preserving camera position, flattening perspective rather than changing spatial relationships. Slow zooms can build tension, while rapid zooms create stylized emphasis.

Best for

- documentaries
- suspense
- sudden discoveries
- retro cinema

Avoid when

- natural perspective change is preferred

Example

A slow zoom isolates the suspect as everyone else fades into the background.

---

## Movement Selection Rules

If emotional intimacy is the priority,

prefer Push In.

If revealing environment is the priority,

prefer Pull Out or Crane.

If following movement,

prefer Tracking Shot or Dolly.

If revealing information,

prefer Pan or Tilt.

If realism is important,

prefer Handheld or Static Camera.

If elegance is desired,

prefer Dolly or Crane.

If movement adds no narrative value,

use a Static Camera.
